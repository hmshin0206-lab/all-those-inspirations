Recently, I watched a video by EO titled [**“How Stanford Teaches AI-Powered Creativity”**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rSS5yM74zeo), which features an interview with Jeremy Utley, a professor at Stanford University. It inspired me in more ways than I expected. It wasn’t just another productivity talk — it felt like an honest, thoughtful guide on how to integrate AI into real life, without feeling overwhelmed or out of touch.

**Key Takeaways**

1\. **Let AI ask you the questions**

One of the most interesting points from the video was this: instead of always asking AI questions, try training it to ask *you* the right ones. Even without any technical background, this mindset shift can open up a new kind of collaboration. For example, Jeremy shared a story about a public park staff member who used AI not to replace their tasks directly, but to help uncover and reframe the most tedious ones — simply by letting the AI dig deeper into the actual problem.

2\. **Don’t treat AI as a tool—treat it like a teammate**

There’s a significant difference in productivity depending on how you relate to AI. When you treat it like a passive tool, you get passive results. But when you treat it like a team member — giving feedback, asking for iterations, challenging its ideas — you start to get much more out of it. AI isn’t just there to do what you say. It can actually help *shape* your thinking, if you let it.

3\. **Stop using AI like a search engine**  
If you’re only asking AI for quick facts, summaries, or links, you’re missing the point. Jeremy suggests moving away from the “Google mindset.” Try using voice input. Let AI interview *you*. Ask it to help structure your thinking. The more you collaborate with it, the more valuable it becomes — not as a database, but as a creative partner.

4\. **Creativity means going past the first idea**

Creativity is about pushing beyond the first thought that comes to mind. AI can help you explore what’s past “good enough.” It’s especially helpful when you’re stuck or when you want to generate new perspectives. It nudges you from “this will do” to “this could be great.”

**My Experience with AI**

These days, I also use AI at work almost daily. Most often, it helps me with translation — not just direct translation, but capturing nuance and tone when switching between Korean and English. It’s the kind of assistance that makes cross-cultural communication feel less stressful, and a little more precise.

But it’s not just language.

Recently, I needed to build a dashboard using a partner’s API. The data had to flow into Google Sheets, and I had never touched Google Console before — let alone written a line of code. Still, I managed to set everything up with AI’s guidance, prompting it step by step. That experience was a big moment for me. It made me realize that AI doesn’t just help you *do* things faster — it actually lets you do things you didn’t think you could.

EO’s interview with Jeremy Utley pushed that realization further. I tried typing the exact same prompts Jeremy mentioned in the video, and AI responded with perspectives I hadn’t considered.

Looking Ahead

Going forward, I want to deepen the way I collaborate with AI — not just to boost efficiency, but to unlock new thinking. It’s already helping me translate better, write faster, and experiment more confidently. But I want to go further: into areas I thought were “not for me” because I didn’t have the technical background.

Maybe even using AI is a creative act — the way we ask, how we respond, the curiosity we bring to the conversation. It’s not just about automation. It’s co-creation.

We’re not just *using* AI anymore — we’re *working with it*.
