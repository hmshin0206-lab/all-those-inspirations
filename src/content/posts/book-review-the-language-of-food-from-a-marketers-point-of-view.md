### Words can signal the “positioning” of your brand (no matter what your intention was)

The author writes that the use of words can tell how cheap or premium your restaurant is. First of all, fine dining restaurants use “more” words. He discovered that statistically, it gets 18 cents more expensive when adding one character to a description of a menu. Not only the number of words but also the quality of words matters. He found out that restaurants that use broad and vague adjectives such as “delicious”, “tasty”, and “amazing” are more likely to be cheap eats. However, high-class restaurants use more specific and fancy words to describe their cuisine.

Thus, if you aim to position your brand as a premium, you should be selective using words to describe it. Otherwise, consumers might be confused or misunderstand. Please refer to the article below on how luxury brands choose vocabularies in order to convey their high-end images:

[Choice Words: How to Speak to the Luxury Customer (Aug 2014, Luxsell)](https://luxsell.me/2014/08/25/choice-words-how-to-speak-to-the-luxury-customer)

### Negative differentiation is everywhere

The author also claims that there are a lot more words to express negativity than positivity in the globe. (If you see restaurant reviews at Yelp, for instance, positive comments are very alike whereas people express their discontent with so many different reasons.) He assumes that this is because people believe that every hardship and difficulty arises from different situations making it hard to group them into one. (And the author calls it ‘negative differentiation’)

> Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.  (Tolstoy, ‘Anna Karenina’)

I think this also explains why numerous brands use negative expressions in their advertisement copies such as “no sugar”, “no trans fat”; to stress out the superiority of the brand and impliedly look down on its competitors simultaneously.

### Making the name of a brand is not (or must not) be an easy-peasy process

It is easy to relate food to the palate and olfactory senses, but sometimes we fail to notice that it is also related to the senses of sights and hearings. For example, people tend to perceive words consist of back vowels like “a”, “o”, and “u” to be “bigger”, “heavier”, and “thicker” than the words with more front vowels like “i” and “e”. In addition, in terms of consonants, the continuants like “m”, “l”, and “r” are associated with images of “soft” and “round”, while strident sounds like “r” and “k” remind you of “jagged” and “sparkling” images. Once you know the pattern, it is no surprise to look around you and see so many ice cream brands named their products using back vowels and continuants.

A name is not just a “name”. It is a “brand identity”, that characterizes its scope and usages, thus it cannot be crafted as if drawing lots, but created throughout fierce contemplations.
